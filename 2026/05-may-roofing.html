The Monthly High-Five

The moves that matter from May 2026.

1. QXO Completes TopBuild Integration — Distribution Consolidation Accelerates

QXO announced completion of its TopBuild integration on May 12, creating a combined entity with $22B+ in annual revenue and 1,200+ distribution locations [1]. 2. DOL Independent Contractor Rule Takes Effect May 11

The Department of Labor's revised independent contractor classification rule became enforceable May 11, 2026 [1]. The six-factor economic reality test now governs 1099 classification across all trades. Early enforcement actions in California and New York targeted roofing contractors with 20+ 1099 crews and minimal documentation. Platforms in diligence are now required to provide trailing 24-month 1099 documentation, worker classification audits, and state-by-state compliance assessments as standard Q&A items.

3. Atlas Roofing Announces June 1 Price Increase — Third Hike in 90 Days

Atlas Roofing issued a May 18 notice of a 4–6% price increase effective June 1, marking the third manufacturer price adjustment since March 23 [1]. CertainTeed and Owens Corning followed with similar notices within 48 hours. The cumulative March-to-June increase now ranges from 11–14% depending on product line. Jobs bid in Q1 at Q1 pricing and scheduled for June installation are facing 12%+ material cost fade before the first shingle is loaded.

4. IEEPA Tariff Refunds Begin Processing — But Relief Is Partial

The first wave of IEEPA tariff refunds began processing the week of May 20, covering eligible imports from the March 12–April 19 window [1]. Industry estimates suggest 15–20% of the March/April tariff burden will be refunded, but Section 232 steel/aluminum tariffs (25%/10%) and Section 301 China tariffs (25%) remain in full effect. Material costs are down 3–4% from April peaks but remain 8–10% above February 2026 levels. The refund is a one-time rebate, not a structural cost reset.

5. CSU Updates 2026 Hurricane Forecast — ACE Revised Down to 85

Colorado State University released its updated 2026 Atlantic hurricane season forecast on May 15, revising the Accumulated Cyclone Energy (ACE) projection down to 85 from the April forecast of 90 [1]. Both figures remain well below the long-term average of 123 and the 2024–2025 hyperactive seasons (ACE 180+ each year). For PE platforms that underwrote acquisition models using 2024–2025 storm-driven EBITDA, the math is now working against them for the second consecutive quarter.

Lessons from the Ladder

History this industry learned from — or repeats.

The 2024–2025 Insurance Restoration Boom — and the 2026 Hangover

The 2024 and 2025 Atlantic hurricane seasons delivered back-to-back years of historic storm activity, with Accumulated Cyclone Energy (ACE) exceeding 180 in both years — more than 45% above the long-term average of 123 [1]. Insurance restoration revenue surged across Florida, Texas, Louisiana, and the Carolinas, and PE platforms that had been circling the roofing sector for years finally pulled the trigger. Deal volume in the roofing contractor space doubled from 2023 to 2025, with purchase price multiples climbing from 5.5–6.5x TTM EBITDA in early 2024 to 7.0–8.5x by Q4 2025 [5].

The thesis was seductive: roofing is recession-resistant, insurance restoration is non-discretionary, and climate change ensures a "new normal" of elevated storm frequency. Platforms were underwritten on trailing-twelve EBITDA that included 18–24 months of hyperactive storm seasons, with limited adjustment for weather normalization. By Q1 2026, the cracks were showing. CSU's April forecast called for an ACE of 90 — below average. The May revision dropped it further to 85 [1]. Suddenly, platforms that had modeled 70–80% insurance restoration revenue were staring at a 2026 where that mix might revert to 40–50%, and the EBITDA that justified the entry multiple was evaporating faster than synergy capture could replace it.

The platforms now facing stress share a common profile: they bought businesses in late 2024 or 2025 at peak multiples, underwrote the deal on peak-year EBITDA, and assumed storm-driven revenue was structural rather than cyclical. The platforms that will survive this cycle did three things differently: (1) they underwrote to normalized weather, not trailing-twelve actuals; (2) they required at least 50% of revenue to come from replacement, reroof, and commercial work independent of storm response; and (3) they built the infrastructure to track Supplement Recovery % and other insurance-specific metrics in real time, so they could see the fade coming before the lenders did.

This isn't the first time the roofing industry has lived this story. The 2017–2019 roll-up wave followed Hurricane Harvey, Irma, and Maria — and by 2020–2021, several Texas and Florida platforms were quietly recapped or sold below entry multiple when storm volume normalized. The lesson is the same now as it was then: weather creates revenue, but diversification creates value. If your platform can't survive at 73% of average ACE, you don't own a roofing business — you own a weather derivative with a tool budget.

The Edge: Supplement Recovery %

The metric that separates insurance restoration operators who survive normalization from those who don't.

What it measures: The percentage of submitted insurance supplements that are approved and paid by the carrier, measured as (Approved Supplement Dollars / Submitted Supplement Dollars) × 100. Tracked monthly and rolled up to trailing-twelve months. A contractor who submits $500K in supplements in May and gets $400K approved has an 80% Supplement Recovery Rate for the month.

Why PE buyers care: Supplement Recovery % is the single cleanest read on whether an insurance restoration business is operationally sophisticated or just riding volume. Any contractor can write an initial estimate and get an insurance check — that's table stakes. The real margin in insurance restoration comes from the supplement: the additional scope discovered during tear-off (hidden rot, code upgrades, ventilation deficiencies) that wasn't in the initial adjuster estimate. Operators with Supplement Recovery % consistently above 75% have documented processes, trained estimators, and adjuster relationships. Operators below 60% are either leaving money on the table or submitting junk supplements that get denied — both are value destroyers post-close.

May 2026 relevance: As storm-driven volume normalizes in 2026, the operators who survive are those who can extract maximum margin from every insurance job they touch. Supplement Recovery % is the leading indicator of that capability. In diligence, PE buyers are now requesting trailing-twelve Supplement Recovery % alongside gain/fade and gross margin by job type [5]. A target with 70%+ Supplement Recovery % and 50%+ non-storm revenue is defensible in a down-weather year. A target with 55% Supplement Recovery % and 75% storm-dependent revenue is a recap waiting to happen.

Watch-out: Supplement Recovery % in isolation can be gamed by only submitting "sure thing" supplements and leaving money on the table. Pair it with Average Supplement $ per Job and Total Supplement $ as % of Revenue. If Supplement Recovery % is high but Total Supplement $ is low, the estimating team is playing it safe rather than maximizing recovery. The best operators have 70–80% Supplement Recovery % and submit supplements on 60–70% of insurance jobs — they're both aggressive and disciplined.

How to track it: Most insurance restoration contractors track supplements in spreadsheets or inside their CRM (AccuLynx, JobNimbus, or Roofr). The key is to capture four data points per job: (1) Initial Estimate $, (2) Supplement Submitted $, (3) Supplement Approved $, and (4) Final Job Revenue $. Roll it up monthly and track the trend. If Supplement Recovery % is declining month-over-month, it's either a carrier crackdown, a documentation problem, or an estimator training gap — all of which are fixable, but only if you're measuring it.

The operators entering Q3 2026 in the strongest position are those who spent May stress-testing their EBITDA model at 73% of average ACE and proving they can still hit margin targets when insurance restoration revenue drops from 75% to 45%. Supplement Recovery % is the metric that proves the margin thesis holds when the weather doesn't cooperate. If you're running an insurance restoration business and you don't know your trailing-twelve Supplement Recovery %, you're flying blind — and if you're a PE buyer evaluating a target and you don't ask for it in Q&A, you're underwriting a weather bet, not a business.

Riddle Answer

Answer: Supplement Recovery %.

In insurance restoration roofing, Supplement Recovery % measures the percentage of submitted insurance supplements that are approved and paid by carriers. When storms hit and volume surges, even mediocre operators can achieve 70–80% recovery rates because adjusters are overwhelmed and approving supplements quickly to clear backlogs. But when weather normalizes and claim volume drops, carriers tighten standards — and Supplement Recovery % reveals which platforms have real operational discipline (documented processes, trained estimators, adjuster relationships) versus those that were just riding the volume wave.

PE buyers now require Supplement Recovery % to stay above 70% and demand that at least 50% of revenue comes from non-storm sources (replacement, reroof, commercial) to prove the business can survive without FEMA declarations. It's the metric that separates platforms built on process from platforms built on weather — and in 2026, with ACE forecasted at 73% of the long-term average, it's the difference between hitting plan and missing covenant.
